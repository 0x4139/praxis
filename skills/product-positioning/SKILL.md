# Product Positioning

Find the thing that makes an offer impossible to ignore — and lead with it. In a crowded market, being safely good is the same as being invisible.

## When to Activate

- Messaging, landing page, or deck copy sounds like every competitor's
- Writing or rewriting a tagline, hero section, or one-liner
- Repositioning an existing product or service
- Prospects say "how are you different from X?" and the answer rambles
- Preparing positioning input for `go-to-market` Step 4 or investor materials

## Core Premise

Most businesses don't need to invent a differentiator — they already have one and are suppressing it because it feels too niche, too risky, or not "professional enough" to lead with. The work is diagnosis and courage, not invention.

Two distinctions drive the whole process:

- **Different vs. better.** "Better service" is a claim every competitor also makes. Positioning is built on what's *different* — something a competitor couldn't honestly say.
- **Claimed vs. observed.** What the founder thinks is distinctive often doesn't show up in how customers actually describe them. Diagnose from real language, not intentions.

## The Process

Work through the three stages in order. Each stage's output feeds the next. Gather the inputs conversationally before producing each deliverable — if the information is too thin to diagnose accurately, ask before concluding.

### Stage 1: Diagnose the Blending Point

Find the specific place where the business sounds like every other option in its space — not "the whole business is generic," the *specific* blending point.

**Inputs to gather:**
- How the user describes what they do, their offer, and who they serve (their actual words)
- What competitors in the space typically say about themselves
- What clients or customers have said about them — in the customers' own words if possible

**How to diagnose:**
- Identify language that is interchangeable with competitors'
- Identify what a customer says about them that they could equally say about three other options
- Separate what the user *thinks* makes them different from what actually shows up in how they're described
- If a description sounds distinct to the user but generic on paper, say so directly
- End the diagnosis with the one specific question Stage 2 must answer for this business — concrete to their situation, not the generic "what makes you different?"

**Rules:**
- Base the diagnosis on what the user actually provides, not assumptions about the industry in general
- Being unremarkable is not a moral failing — name it plainly without dramatizing it
- Too little input to diagnose accurately? Ask, don't guess

**Deliverable:**

```
The Blending Point:       [The specific place you sound like everyone else]
Interchangeable Language: [Words or claims you share with competitors]
What This Costs You:      [Why blending in here is actually hurting you]
The Real Question:        [What you need to answer before moving forward]
```

### Stage 2: Surface What's Being Downplayed

Find the thing about the business that's already true but gets left out of the pitch because it felt too unconventional, too niche, or too much.

**Inputs to gather:**
- The Stage 1 diagnosis
- Something about how the user works that feels too unconventional to mention upfront
- Feedback they've received that their approach is "different" or "not typical"

**How to surface it:**
- Ask what they do differently that they usually leave out of their own description, and why
- Look for anything they were told was "too niche" or "too much" and toned down instead
- Separate what would make them sound *better* from what would make them sound *different* — only the latter counts

**Rules:**
- The answer must be something already true about the business, not a new idea to invent
- Never accept a generic strength ("better service", "more personal") — it must be specific
- Nothing suppressed is obvious? Ask directly: "What have you been told to tone down?"
- If, after probing, nothing genuinely different exists, say so — the honest output is "you don't have a positioning problem, you have a differentiation problem," and the next step is product/offer work (see `go-to-market` Steps 1–3), not messaging

**Deliverable:**

```
What's Being Downplayed:       [The specific thing, not a generic strength]
Why It Was Suppressed:         [The likely reason it was toned down]
Different vs Better:           [Confirming this is genuinely different, not just an improvement]
What Happens If You Lead With It: [The realistic upside and the realistic risk]
```

### Stage 3: Build the Positioning Move

Turn the suppressed differentiator into one specific, executable move — not vague advice to "be bolder."

**Inputs to gather:**
- The Stage 2 output
- Where people currently first encounter the business or offer (homepage, profile, referral conversation, cold email)

**How to build it:**
- Decide where the differentiator should show up first: the messaging, the offer itself, or how conversations open
- Design one concrete way to make it the first thing people notice
- Identify what currently buries it — that has to stop
- Define what success looks like: what would people repeating it *unprompted* actually sound like for this business?

**Rules:**
- The move must be specific and executable, not "be more confident"
- It must be realistic to implement with existing people and budget — no rebrand, no new hires
- If leading with the differentiator carries real risk, name the risk honestly rather than ignoring it
- The move must directly use the specific thing from Stage 2, not a generic differentiation tactic

**Deliverable:**

```
Your Positioning Move:  [Specific, concrete, executable]
Where It Shows Up First: [Messaging, offer, or conversation opener]
Stop Doing This:        [What currently buries the remarkable thing]
What Success Looks Like: [What unprompted word of mouth would actually look like]
```

## Deliverable Format

When all three stages are complete, produce a single positioning brief containing:

1. **The diagnosis** (Stage 1) — where and why the current messaging blends in
2. **The differentiator** (Stage 2) — what's genuinely different and why it was hidden
3. **The move** (Stage 3) — the one change to make, where, and how to know it worked

Keep it short. This is a decision document, not a strategy deck — the `go-to-market` skill covers the full strategy.

If the user asked for specific copy (tagline, hero section, one-liner), write it after the brief, leading with the Stage 2 differentiator — the brief is the input, not the final answer.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Inventing a differentiator** — if it isn't already true, it's a product roadmap item, not positioning
- **"Better" masquerading as "different"** — faster, cheaper, friendlier are improvements competitors can match, not positions
- **Diagnosing from industry stereotypes** — the analysis must come from the user's actual language and customers' actual words
- **Generic bravery advice** — "be bolder", "own your niche", "show personality" are not executable moves
- **The whole-business verdict** — "your positioning is generic" is useless; name the one specific blending point
- **Ignoring the risk** — leading with something unconventional has a cost; name it instead of pretending it's free

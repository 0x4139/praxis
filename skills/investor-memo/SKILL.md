# Investment Memo

Write a memo that makes the investment case clear, honest, and defensible.

## When to Activate

- Writing a memo to send to prospective investors
- Preparing for a partner meeting (the memo is what gets passed around the table)
- Reviewing your own pitch through the investor's lens
- Summarizing a company for an internal investment committee

## Two Formats

**Founder memo** — you write this to make the case for your company. Sent to investors before or after a meeting. Tone: confident but honest. Goal: get to the next meeting.

**VC-internal memo** — investors write this to recommend (or pass on) a deal to their partners. Understanding this format helps you anticipate what investors will scrutinize and pre-answer their objections.

Both follow the same structure. The difference is voice and emphasis.

## Memo Structure

### 1. Executive Summary

Two to three sentences max. An investor should know what you do, why it matters, and what you're raising after reading this.

```
[Company] is a [category] that [what it does] for [who].
[Key traction metric]. Raising [amount] on [instrument] to [what the money does].
```

**Example:**
> Acme is a developer tool that automates database migrations for teams running PostgreSQL at scale. 400 teams actively using the product, $180K ARR growing 25% MoM. Raising $3M on a SAFE to hire engineering and launch enterprise tier.

### 2. Company Overview

Brief context on who you are before diving into the team and problem.

| Field | What to include |
|-------|----------------|
| Founded | Year, city |
| Stage | Pre-seed / Seed / Series A |
| Mission | One sentence — what you exist to do |
| Key milestones | 3-5 bullets: launch dates, partnerships, funding history, product-market fit signals |
| Legal structure | Entity type, incorporation state/country |

**Rules:**
- Keep this to half a page max — it's context, not the pitch
- Milestones should show momentum, not a timeline of existence
- If you've raised before, state the amount, date, and lead investor

### 3. Team

Investors fund teams first, especially at early stages.

| Field | What to include |
|-------|----------------|
| Founders | Name, role, relevant background (not full resume) |
| Founder-market fit | Why THIS team for THIS problem? |
| Key hires | Notable early employees if relevant |
| Gaps | Be honest — what roles do you need to fill? |

**What investors look for:**
- Domain expertise or lived experience with the problem
- Previous startup experience (even failed ones)
- Technical depth if it's a technical product
- Complementary skills between co-founders

### 4. Problem

State the problem precisely. Quantify the pain.

**Template:**
```
Who: [specific customer segment]
Problem: [what they struggle with]
Current solution: [how they handle it today]
Why it fails: [specific shortcoming]
Cost of the problem: [time, money, risk — quantified]
```

**Rules:**
- Describe the problem from the customer's perspective, not yours
- Use real quotes from interviews if you have them
- Quantify: "costs teams 8 hours/week" beats "wastes a lot of time"
- The problem should feel urgent, not theoretical

### 5. Solution

Show how your product solves the problem. Be specific.

- Lead with the outcome, not the technology
- Show, don't tell — screenshots, demo flow, or a concrete before/after
- Explain your insight: what do you understand about this problem that others don't?
- Keep it to one page — the product demo does the heavy lifting

### 6. Market

Size the opportunity. Investors need to believe this can be big enough.

| Level | Definition | How to calculate |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|
| TAM | Total addressable market | Everyone who *could* use this |
| SAM | Serviceable addressable market | The segment you can realistically reach |
| SOM | Serviceable obtainable market | What you can capture in 3-5 years |

**Rules:**
- Bottom-up sizing is more credible than top-down ("$50B market" means nothing)
- Bottom-up: [# of potential customers] x [annual contract value] = SAM
- Show the market is growing — cite a source
- If the market is small today, explain why it's expanding (new regulation, tech shift, behavior change)

### 7. Business Model

How does money flow?

| Field | Answer |
|-------|--------|
| Revenue model | SaaS / usage / marketplace / transactional |
| Pricing | Tiers, per-seat, per-unit — be specific |
| ACV | Average contract value |
| Gross margin | Revenue minus COGS |
| Unit economics | CAC, LTV, LTV:CAC ratio, payback period |
| Expansion | How do customers pay you more over time? |

**If pre-revenue:** describe the planned model and comparable pricing from similar products. Acknowledge it's unvalidated.

### 8. Traction

The single most important section for investors. Show momentum.

**Metrics to include (stage-dependent):**

| Stage | Key metrics |
|-------|------------|
| Pre-launch | Waitlist, LOIs, design partners, pilot commitments |
| Post-launch, pre-revenue | Active users, growth rate, engagement, retention |
| Revenue | MRR/ARR, growth rate, churn, NDR, pipeline |

**Rules:**
- Show the trend, not just the snapshot (graph > single number)
- Month-over-month growth rate matters more than absolute numbers at early stage
- Cohort retention is stronger than vanity user counts
- If growth is lumpy, explain why honestly

### 9. Competition

Every company has competition. Saying "no competitors" is a red flag.

**Competitive matrix:**

| Competitor | Approach | Strength | Weakness | Your advantage |
|-----------|----------|----------|----------|----------------|
| Direct competitor | | | | |
| Indirect competitor | | | | |
| Status quo (spreadsheets, manual) | | | | |

**Rules:**
- Include the status quo as a competitor — it's often the biggest one
- Be fair to competitors — investors will check
- Your advantage should be structural (network effects, data moats, distribution), not just "better product"
- If a big company could build this, explain why they won't or why you'd win anyway

### 10. Go-To-Market

How do you acquire and retain customers?

- Current channels and what's working
- Sales motion (self-serve, sales-assisted, enterprise)
- Customer acquisition cost and how it trends
- Expansion strategy (land-and-expand, upsell, cross-sell)

Keep this concise — link to the full GTM strategy document if you have one.

### 11. Financials

Show you understand the economics of your business.

**Include:**
- Current burn rate and runway
- Revenue projections (12-24 months, with assumptions visible)
- Path to breakeven or next fundraise
- Key assumptions that drive the model

**Rules:**
- Projections should be ambitious but defensible — investors will stress-test them
- Show the assumptions, not just the outputs
- "We'll be at $10M ARR in 18 months" needs a clear bottoms-up path
- Bear / base / bull cases show maturity

### 12. The Ask

What you're raising and what it buys.

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| Amount | |
| Instrument | SAFE / priced round / convertible |
| Valuation (if priced) | |
| Use of funds | Top 3-4 categories with % allocation |
| Milestones this round enables | What will be true 18 months from now? |
| Target close date | |

**Use of funds example:**
```
60% — Engineering (3 senior hires)
20% — Sales (first 2 AEs + tooling)
15% — Infrastructure (scale to 10x current load)
 5% — Operations
```

### 13. Risks and Mitigations

Naming your risks builds credibility. Hiding them doesn't.

| Risk | Severity | Mitigation |
|------|----------|------------|
| | High / Medium / Low | |

**Common risk categories:**
- Market risk — is the market real and big enough?
- Execution risk — can the team build and ship?
- Technology risk — can it actually work at scale?
- Regulatory risk — could regulation help or kill this?
- Competition risk — what if [big company] builds this?
- Funding risk — what happens if this round takes longer?

### 14. Investment Thesis (VC-Internal)

This section is what the deal champion writes to convince their partners. If you're a founder, write this section as if you were the VC advocating for your deal.

```
We should invest in [company] because:
1. [Market insight] — why this market, why now
2. [Team edge] — why this team wins
3. [Traction signal] — what the data says
4. [Structural advantage] — moat or unfair advantage
5. [Return path] — how this becomes a fund-returning outcome
```

## Formatting Rules

- Keep the full memo to 3-6 pages (not 20)
- Use tables and bullet points, not paragraphs
- Lead every section with the conclusion, then the evidence
- Every number needs a source or an explicit assumption label
- Bold the key takeaway in each section for skimmers (investors skim)

## Anti-Patterns

- **"We have no competition"** — you do, even if it's spreadsheets and manual processes
- **Top-down market sizing only** — "$50B TAM" without bottoms-up math is hand-waving
- **Hockey stick projections with no driver** — show what causes the growth, not just the shape
- **Burying the traction** — lead with your strongest metrics, don't hide them on page 4
- **Omitting risks** — investors will find them; better to name them and show mitigations
- **Inconsistent numbers** — if the deck says $200K ARR and the memo says $180K, you're done
- **Writing a novel** — if it's over 6 pages, cut it in half

## Quality Gate

Before sending:
- [ ] Executive summary works standalone — investor gets it without reading further
- [ ] Every metric matches the pitch deck and financial model
- [ ] Assumptions are labeled, not hidden in projections
- [ ] Risks section is honest and includes mitigations
- [ ] The ask is specific (amount, instrument, use of funds, milestones)
- [ ] A skeptical reader can follow the logic without hand-waving
- [ ] Under 6 pages
